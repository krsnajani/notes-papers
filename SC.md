# Constitutl Theory

In comparative constitutional theory, constituent assemblies are fundamentally shaped by how delegates are selected and the upstream procedural and substantive constraints imposed by convening authorities. In both the United States and post-war Germany, delegates were appointed by state legislatures, with the Allied occupying powers in Germany mandating that delegate allocation be strictly proportional to state populations—a principle also seen in India’s Constituent Assembly, which allocated roughly one representative per ten lakh people. By contrast, delegates to the 1789 French Estates-General were divided among the Three Estates, with the Crown granting the Third Estate doubled representation to equal the clergy and nobility combined. Beyond delegate selection, upstream actors—such as legislatures, monarchs, or occupying forces—routinely attempt to bind constituent bodies through procedural rules or substantive mandates. In the United States, the Continental Congress explicitly instructed the Philadelphia Convention merely to revise the Articles of Confederation rather than replace them, while small states like Delaware bound their delegates to safeguard equal state voting power. In France, upstream mandates from conservative factions and the Crown sought an absolute royal veto and insisted on voting by estate rather than by head to neutralize the Third Estate's numerical advantage. Similarly, in post-war Germany, the Allied powers imposed a strict substantive constraint requiring a decentralized, federal structure that empowered the states (Länder) while intentionally limiting central federal authority to prevent the resurgence of an authoritarian regime.

Moving beyond upstream limits, downstream constraints emerge from the requirement of ratification, where the draft constitution must be approved by an external body, the electorate, or sub-national entities. Because constitution-makers anticipate the preferences of those who must ratify the document, their framing choices are significantly constrained by what will realistically be accepted. While some bodies—such as India’s Constituent Assembly—functioned as sovereign, self-ratifying conventions that did not submit their final document to an external third party or popular referendum, the United States presents a classic case of downstream pressure. Under Article VII of the U.S. Constitution, enactment required ratification by conventions in at least nine of the thirteen states. Aware that small states would outright reject the document if their autonomy was erased, the framers at Philadelphia were forced to accommodate their demands for equal state sovereignty. This downstream pressure directly produced the Great Compromise embodied in Article I: while the House of Representatives reflects population, the Senate guarantees equal representation with two senators per state regardless of size (amounting to 100 senators across today's 50 states). Ultimately, downstream constraints ensure that the viability of a constitution is dictated not only by the intentions of its authors, but by the strategic compromises necessary to secure its final adoption.

In constitutional design, downstream constraints operate through the necessity of external ratification, forcing framers to anticipate and satisfy the preferences of ratifying authorities. In France (1789), the King initially claimed a veto over constitutional provisions, compelling the National Assembly to heed royal interests, while in post-war Germany, the Allied powers reserved the ultimate right to approve the Basic Law before state ratification, effectively barring framers from establishing an overly centralized federal state. However, theorists like Jon Elster note that the boundary between upstream and downstream constraints is often blurred—especially when the downstream veto is wielded by the very upstream authority that convened the assembly—and the concept of a "constraint" itself can be deeply ambiguous. An apparent external constraint is misleading if the assembly would have chosen that path regardless. For example, while some argue the Government of India Act of 1935 constrained India toward a strong central government, India's framers voluntarily adopted that strong center to preserve national integrity post-Partition, meaning it was not a true constraint. Furthermore, assemblies often actively subvert their mandates. As constitutional scholar Sir K.C. Wheare observed regarding the Indian Constituent Assembly, although it was convened under the British Cabinet Mission Plan with specific procedural limitations and expected imperial oversight, the Assembly declared its own plenary sovereignty, bypassed imperial ratification, and enacted the Constitution under its own authority (repealing the imperial statutes under Article 395). In Wheare’s formulation of constitutional autochthony, this deliberate break in legal continuity constituted a technically extra-legal or "illegal" act from the standpoint of the prior imperial legal order, ensuring the new constitution derived its ultimate authority entirely from domestic soil.

In examining how constituent assemblies navigate downstream ratification, the framers routinely defied the explicit instructions of their upstream creators. As Jon Elster highlights in Forces and Mechanisms in the Constitution-Making Process[1], the Philadelphia Convention broke its mandate on three critical points: they drafted an entirely new constitution, substituted popular state ratifying conventions for state legislatures, and required ratification by nine out of thirteen states rather than the unanimous consent demanded by the Articles of Confederation[1]. Similarly, the French National Assembly ignored constituency instructions (cahiers) regarding voting procedures and the royal veto, while the German Parliamentary Council in 1948 successfully resisted Allied pressure for a popular referendum, insisting instead on ratification by state parliaments (Landtage) to avoid communist propaganda and political instability. The German framers also pushed back against extreme decentralization, securing a more viable, cohesive federal administration[1]. Elster summarizes this dynamic through two opposing slogans: "Let the kingmaker beware of the king" versus "Let the king beware of the kingmaker," where the "king" (the assembly) consistently triumphed over the "kingmaker" (the convening authorities). This dynamic extends directly to India: rather than acting under external imperial constraints, the Indian framers deliberately opted for a strong centralized union and cooperative federalism out of their own strategic imperative—to prevent further balkanization and preserve national unity and integrity in the traumatic aftermath of Partition.

In analyzing the internal dynamics of constitution-making, Jon Elster distinguishes between the actual motivations of the framers and their motivational assumptions about future actors (such as voters, politicians, and judges)[1]. Framers typically viewed themselves as guided by "loftier motives"—impartial reason and public interest—while assuming future political actors would be driven by base self-interest[1]. At the 1787 Philadelphia Convention, James Madison operationalized the famous Humean maxim that "every man must be supposed a knave," designing institutional checks, balances, and anti-factional mechanisms on the premise that future politicians would pursue narrow, partisan power rather than the common good. When analyzing these underlying motives, Elster categorizes them into interest, passion, and reason, further dividing interest into personal, group, and institutional categories[1]. While historians largely reject Charles Beard’s famous thesis that the American framers were driven primarily by personal financial gain[3], group interests exerted undeniable influence[1]. This is evident in the compromises between Northern commercial states and Southern agrarian slaveholders over the Three-Fifths Clause and the protection of the slave trade until 1808. A parallel accommodation of group interests occurred in the Indian Constitution: the framers integrated specific structural concessions—such as Article 291 (guaranteeing privy purses to princely rulers to secure integration) and Articles 331 and 333 (providing reserved legislative representation for the Anglo-Indian minority)—demonstrating that constitutional bargaining consistently reflects the strategic accommodation of distinct group interests and vulnerable communities.

While constitution-makers frequently profess disinterested public service, their decisions can be distinctly shaped by personal and institutional self-interest—designing constitutional offices to safeguard their own political futures. As Jon Elster illustrates, the starkest contemporary example occurred during the 1992 dissolution of Czechoslovakia: the Czech National Council created a bicameral parliament in the new constitution specifically to offer seats in a newly established Senate as an incentive to Czech deputies in the Federal Assembly, securing their votes to legally dissolve the federation. Beyond such personal political bargains, however, bicameralism serves a crucial structural function in counteracting executive dominance in parliamentary democracies. Because the executive in a parliamentary system is formed by the legislative majority, a unicameral system risks unchecked executive power. In the Indian context, when a government secures a decisive single-party majority in the Lok Sabha (as seen following the 2014 general elections), the executive exercises virtually unbridled legislative power in the lower house. This contrasts sharply with coalition eras—such as Atal Bihari Vajpayee’s NDA government between 1999 and 2004—where the cabinet was continuously constrained by coalition partners. A bicameral legislature, through an upper house like the Rajya Sabha, provides an indispensable counterweight: representing regional state interests, slowing down hasty legislation, and ensuring that a dominant lower-house majority cannot bypass constitutional deliberation.
