# PART I: DETAILED CASE ANALYSES

### 1. The Swiss Annulment Case: Lapsed Arbitral Deadline
*(Swiss Federal Supreme Court, addressing Swiss PILA Art. 190)*
* **Factual Background:** 
  The arbitral proceedings were closed, but the parties waited approximately two years and four months without an award. Despite repeated promises by the arbitrator to act diligently, no award was forthcoming. The parties eventually agreed upon a strict, final ultimatum deadline: **September 2, 2013, at 5:00 p.m.** The arbitrator missed this deadline by one day, rendering the award on **September 3, 2013**.
* **The Challenge:**
  The Claimant (Appellant) petitioned the Swiss Federal Supreme Court to annul the award. The Respondent opposed the petition, arguing:
  1. *Procedural opportunism:* Claimant only challenged the award because the outcome on the merits was unfavorable.
  2. *Lack of legal basis:* Claimant mistakenly framed its argument under Swiss Private International Law Act (PILA) Art. 190(2)(a) (irregular appointment/composition of tribunal).
* **Court’s Findings & Holding:**
  * **No Abuse of Rights:** The court rejected the “opportunism” argument. Patience has limits; the parties waited over two years and issued an explicit ultimatum. Setting aside the award based on the arbitrator's failure to adhere to the ultimatum was not abusive.
  * **Jurisdiction *Ratione Temporis* (Art. 190(2)(b) PILA):** While the Appellant mistakenly pleaded Art. 190(2)(a), the court held that dismissing the appeal on this ground alone would be excessively formalistic. It held that the proper ground was **Art. 190(2)(b) PILA** (*wrongly accepting or declining jurisdiction*): **the arbitrator's mandate and adjudicatory powers expired with the deadline**, depriving the arbitrator of jurisdiction to render an award. The award was annulled.

### 2. *Buyer (Mozambique) v. Seller (The Netherlands)* (*Seed Potatoes*)
*(Preliminary Award, ICC Case No. 5505 of 1987, Arbitrator Georges Muller; Lausanne, Switzerland)*
* **Factual Background:**
  A contract for the sale of seed potatoes contained an Annex II arbitration clause:
  > *“Both parties undertake to fulfill this contract in good faith. Any dispute... should be settled through an amicable negotiation. Should no agreement be arrived at, they must finally undertake to submit the matter according to the regulation for agreement and arbitration of the International Chamber of Commerce to one or more arbitrators as per the said laws. The arbitration will take place in Switzerland, the law applicable is that known in England.”*
* **The Dispute:**
  The Claimant asserted the clause chose **English substantive law**. The Defendant argued the clause was ambiguous/invalid as a choice of substantive law and that **Dutch substantive law** applied.
* **Arbitrator’s Multi-Step Analytical Framework:**
  1. **Interpretation Standard:** Under Swiss law (*lex arbitri/lex fori*) and general principles, contracts are construed according to the principle of good faith—how a reasonable international commercial party would understand the words.
  2. **Evaluating the Four Possible Meanings of “Law Applicable”:**
     * *Choice of Procedural Law?* **No.** Parties arbitrate precisely to escape local court procedures. Procedural needs were already satisfied by the ICC Rules and the mandatory rules of the Swiss seat. Choosing English procedure in a Swiss seat would be extraordinary and cause needless friction.
     * *Choice of Conflict-of-Laws Rules?* **No.** Commercial parties do not intentionally select a foreign conflicts system without choosing the underlying substantive law.
     * *Choice of Law Governing the Arbitration Agreement?* **No.** Parties rarely choose a law specifically for the arbitration agreement separate from the main contract or seat unless expressly identified.
     * *Choice of Substantive Law?* **Yes.** In international business usage, the term "applicable law" universally refers to the substantive law governing the merits.
  3. **Validity of Choosing English Law without Territorial Connection:**
     * The Defendant argued that the choice was invalid because the dispute lacked any factual connection to England.
     * The arbitrator held that both Swiss and English law recognize wide party autonomy: **a factual/natural connection is not required**. Commercial parties may choose a neutral third-country law (such as English law) because of its sophistication, neutrality, and suitability for international trade.

### 3. *A (South Africa) v. B (U.S.A.)*
*(Swiss Federal Supreme Court, Decision No. 4A 240/2009)*
* **Factual Background:**
  A cross-border supply contract contained a choice-of-law clause providing that the agreement:
  > *“…shall be construed and interpreted in accordance with the laws of Switzerland as applied between domestic parties provided, however, that the express agreements... shall always prevail.”*
  The contract provided a right to terminate in the event of an uncured **“material breach.”**
* **The Arbitral Tribunal's Decision:**
  The tribunal found that B failed to perform certain obligations, but this did not constitute a “material breach.” Because the concept of “material breach” is native to common law and **unknown to domestic Swiss sales law**, the tribunal defined the term by looking to **Article 25 of the CISG** (fundamental breach) and **Article 7.3.1 of the UNIDROIT Principles**.
* **Annulment Challenge before the Swiss Supreme Court:**
  The South African party sought annulment on two grounds under the Swiss PILA:
  1. *Excess of Mandate (Art. 190(2)(c)):* The tribunal disregarded the parties' express instruction to apply Swiss domestic law (which excluded the CISG).
  2. *Violation of the Right to be Heard / Surprise (Art. 190(2)(d)):* The tribunal applied the CISG and UNIDROIT Principles *sua sponte* without allowing the parties to brief them.
* **Supreme Court Holding:**
  * **No Excess of Mandate:** The tribunal did not apply an excluded foreign law as governing law; rather, it applied **Swiss rules of contract interpretation** (which require determining the objective intent and understanding of reasonable commercial parties in an international transaction). It was entirely consistent with Swiss law to interpret an Anglo-American legal term inserted into an international contract by referring to international instruments like the CISG and UNIDROIT Principles.
  * **No Violation of Right to be Heard:** Interpreting an international term according to standard international practice could not have come as a surprise to the parties. The challenge was dismissed.

### 4. *Seller (Korea) v. Buyer (Jordan)*
*(Interim Award, ICC Case No. 6149 of 1990; Paris seat)*
* **Factual Background:**
  A Korean seller and a Jordanian buyer contracted for goods to be delivered in installments to Iraq. The contract provided for ICC arbitration in Paris, but contained **no choice of substantive law**. 
* **Preliminary Issue: Jordanian Statutory Prohibition on Foreign Jurisdiction:**
  The buyer challenged the jurisdiction/validity of the arbitration clause based on **Section 2 of Jordanian Law No. 35 of 1983**, which provided that any agreement barring Jordanian courts from hearing disputes relating to bills of lading/carriage of goods is null and void.
  * *Tribunal's Analysis:* The tribunal held that Section 2 of the Jordanian law did not apply. Whether under the doctrine that the arbitration clause shares the proper law of the contract (Korean law) or that it is governed by the law of the seat/place of award (French law, supported by Art. V(1)(a) of the New York Convention), the clause was valid. 
  * A tribunal sitting in Paris is **not bound by Jordanian domestic public policy**, especially when parties purposefully agreed on an ICC forum to withdraw disputes from national courts.
* **Merits: Determination of the Applicable Substantive Law:**
  Applying Article 13(3) of the 1988 ICC Rules (which directs the arbitrator to apply the proper law indicated by the rule of conflict deemed appropriate), the tribunal tested the dispute under multiple approaches:
  1. *Cumulative / Harmonized Conflicts Rules:* The conflict-of-laws rules of all connected jurisdictions—Korea (place of contracting/seller), Jordan (buyer), Iraq (place of delivery), and France (seat)—unanimously pointed to the substantive law of the **place where the contract was concluded (Korea)**.
  2. *Modern Transnational Conflict Principles:* Under the 1955 Hague Convention on the Law Applicable to International Sales of Goods (Art. 3) and the 1980 Rome Convention (Art. 4), contracts are presumed most closely connected to the country of the party performing the **characteristic performance** (the **seller’s domestic law**).
  * *Result:* Under every applicable conflict-of-laws analysis, **Korean substantive law** governed.
* **Rejection of the Claimant's *Lex Mercatoria* / CISG Argument:**
  The claimant urged the tribunal to bypass domestic law and apply *lex mercatoria* (specifically the CISG). The tribunal rejected this:
  * Even if *lex mercatoria* were viable, it is incomplete. The dispute involved claims of **unjust enrichment** and **statutes of limitation**, which are **not addressed by the CISG**. Recourse to a comprehensive national legal system (Korean law) was inevitable.

# PART II: THEORETICAL & DOCTRINAL FRAMEWORKS ("AND OTHERS")

### 1. The Tripartite Framework of Law in International Arbitration
The materials emphasize that unlike domestic litigation, an arbitral tribunal has no natural *forum* and no built-in *lex fori*. Arbitrators must navigate three separate bodies of law:
1. **The *Lex Arbitri* (Curial Law):** The national law of the arbitral seat supervising the proceedings, providing default procedural rules, supporting the arbitral tribunal (court assistance), and governing actions to set aside/annul awards.
2. **The Law Governing the Arbitration Agreement:** Determines formal/substantive validity and arbitrability (frequently decided under the law chosen by the parties, the law of the seat, or transnational principles).
3. **The Substantive Law Governing the Merits:** The law determining the rights and duties of the parties under the commercial contract.

---

### 2. Substantive Law: Comparative Statutory Models
The text contrasts three foundational formulations:

| Instrument | Party Choice | Absence of Party Choice | Key Characteristics |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1961 Geneva Convention (Art. VII)** | Freedom to determine the *law*. | Arbitrators apply the proper law under the *rule of conflict* they deem applicable. | Retains traditional private international law conflict-of-laws analysis; respects trade usages and contract terms. |
| **2017 ICC Rules (Art. 21)** | Freedom to agree upon *rules of law*. | Arbitrators directly apply the *rules of law* determined to be appropriate (*voie directe*). | Modern and flexible; bypasses conflict-of-laws systems; allows non-national rules (e.g., UNIDROIT Principles). |
| **UNCITRAL Model Law (Art. 28)** | Freedom to choose *rules of law*. | Arbitrators apply the *law* determined by the *conflict of laws rules* they consider applicable (*voie indirecte*). | Compromise approach: gives broad autonomy to parties, but binds tribunals to conflicts rules to prevent arbitrariness. |

* **"Law" vs. "Rules of Law":**
  * As Holtzmann & Neuhaus explain, using the phrase *"rules of law"* rather than *"law"* was a deliberate policy choice in the Model Law and ICC Rules. It empowers parties to choose rules that are not complete national codes, such as international conventions not yet in force (e.g., draft conventions), non-state rules, or to select different national laws for different parts of the contract (*dépeçage*).

### 3. Delocalization vs. The Jurisdictional Theory
*(Von Mehren & Jiménez de Aréchaga Report, 1989)*
* **The Inadequacy of the Jurisdictional Theory:** 
  Historically, arbitration was viewed under the *jurisdictional theory*, which held that arbitrations derive their binding authority entirely from the sovereign territory where they sit, imposing the local court's *lex fori*.
* **The Contemporary Reality (Ambulatory Arbitration):**
  * The seat of arbitration is typically chosen for geographical convenience, neutral ground, and logistical facilities—not because the parties wanted the local state's procedural and choice-of-law doctrines.
  * Forcing the local *lex fori* onto an international tribunal produces arbitrary results and defeats party autonomy. 
  * Modern international arbitration is largely **delocalized and ambulatory**. Sovereignty primarily becomes relevant only at the end stage: **enforcement under the New York Convention**.

---

### 4. The Dilemma of Mandatory Rules and Arbitrability
* Arbitrators are private adjudicators whose mandate derives from party consent, not state sovereigns. 
* **The Dilemma:** When an arbitration agreement subjects disputes to Country B's law, but the mandatory law / public policy of Country A (such as antitrust law or carriage-of-goods statutes like Jordan's Law 35) claims exclusive jurisdiction:
  * Applying foreign mandatory rules risks defying party autonomy and exceeding the tribunal's mandate.
  * Ignoring foreign mandatory rules risks rendering the eventual award unenforceable under Article V(2) of the New York Convention in countries where enforcement must be sought.
